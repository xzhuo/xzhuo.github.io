## Background

The battle of Cannae doesn't make sense. Two hundred years before it, Sun Tzu famously said: "if ten times the enemy's strength, surround them". However, against ~80k Roman troops, Hannibal only had ~50k of Carthaginian troops at Cannae. It is against basic military principle, and almost physically impossible to encircle a numerically superior army with such a small force. And back in 216BC, Hannibal did not have any of those so-called "force multipliers". Despite it, Hannibal somehow did the impossible: he surrounded the bulk of the Roman legions with a smaller army and annihilated them.

There are countless myths surrounding ancient events, but the force comparison and outcome of this battle have been well-documented and leaves little room for dispute. So the question remains: what exactly happened on the battlefield at Cannae?

If you search online, read Wikipedia, and watch related YouTube videos, the general consensus is that Hannibal employed a “crescent-shaped” front to engage Roman infantry. While holding both wings, they performed a controlled retreat from the center to form a concave line, effectively sinking the Roman infantry into an encirclement. However, I disagree with this description. The conventional depiction overemphasized how they could form the encirclement, but neglected to consider whether the Carthaginian troops had enough strength to withhold the encirclement. And the notion that back row Roman soldiers rushed to the center is dubious.

In fact, I am not the only one thinking about the problem here. There is a great Youtube video talking about the problem of the battle and using the real size of the battle formation (2 km wide, only a few hundreds deep) to highlight how improbable the conventional description is.

I have the conventional view illustrated here in Figure 1 and there are some other problems.

1. Why did the Roman ignore Hannibal's flanking African troops? It is common sense to protect the flanks of advancing formation. By the conventional view, the Hannibal's African troops can be clearly seen at the beginning of the battle. Even if the Roman want to breakthrough from the center, they would still deploy some troops to protect the flank, and they have enough strength to do that. Apparently Roman made big mistakes in the battle, but it is unlikely that they will violate the basic common sense at the planning phase.  
2. Polybius described "the line of the flanking companies growing thinner as it was prolonged". So the flanking companies of his central formation is thinner than the center units. However, the crescent-to-concave transformation requires his weaker, flanking companies to held their position while the center retreat more. It requires too much from his Hispanics and celts unit, especially the flanking companies. Roman has the best contemperary heavey infantry, and they have superior numbers on that day.
3. The final concave battle line is not structrually sound to withhold pressure and it is too weak to withhold the Roman heavy infantry. The forward momentum of Roman infantry (~50k) advance is stopped, and held in a concaved line by Hannibal's center formation (~25k). It is very tall task and the concaved defense line made it even more difficult tactically. From the final position, Hannibal's line can't give an inch. Every step Roman line moved forward will stretch the already thinned defense line more, made the next push easier. Meanwhile, the Roman line has enough number and density to sustain continued push, and they are better equipped! I just can't imagine how Hannibal can stop the Roman in such a way.

One argument is that the Roman legions were formed with inexperienced soldiers, making them less combat-effective. However, the situation the Romans found themselves in was precisely what Sun Tzu referred to as “death ground”: there was no escape route, and soldiers would face certain death without achieving victory. Sun Tzu actually advocate to place soldiers in such perilous positions to prevent escape and instill a fighting spirit. The point is, regardless of whether they were green troops or not, all soldiers would fight with unwavering determination to survive once encircled.

In my opinion, the key to Hannibal's victory was not just the encirclement, but how he disoriented the Roman formation to render them combat ineffective. I will illustrate my understanding here and show why his crescent-shaped front is critical in achieving it.

## the Battle of Trebia

To understand Hannibal's tactics, we have to go back to the Battle of Trebia. Hannibal had two major battles with the Rome before Cannae: Trebia and Lake Trasimene. The latter one was an ambush and less relevant. But Trebia revealed the Rome's core strength and doctrine on the battlefield. In brief, Hannibal almost pulled a hammer and anvil to the Romans and its accessories, but the bulk of Roman legions, led by the Roman consul Sempronius Longus,  punched through the "anvil" (Carthaginian infantry line) and left the battlefield in formation. As seen in the figure from Wikipedia.

![Fig. 2: The battle of Trebia illustration from wikipedia](/assets/img/Battle_of_the_Trebia,_forces_engaged.png){: width="750" }

Fig. 2: The battle of Trebia (from <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Trebia>).

At Trebia, Roman legions demonstrated why they were the best contemporary heavy infantry. Disciplined and well-equipped, it is hard to match and held their steady advance -- Hannibal's infantry apparently failed to do so and suffered heavy casualties at Trebia. Even when outflanked and facing the danger of being encircled, they never flee just kept attacking forward until break the enemies line. Despite suffering a defeat, Roman infantry was something Hannibal had to reckon with.

I will build my thesis on these assumptions:

1. Roman legions were the best contemporary heavy infantry and it was almost impossible to withhold their battle line from moving forward.
2. Roman soldiers were well-disciplined to maintain their continuous line and won't flee in mass.
3. The battle front is too wide for the inexperienced Roman commander to control, and he would be close to the front line and slow to make adjustments.

Contradictory to most models, I think the Carthaginian units in the crescent front formed a continuous curved line, instead of a series of staggered short straight lines facing the enemy (cite wiki and west point). According to Polybius, "the line of the flanking companies growing thinner as it was prolonged." So the crescent Carthaginian line was not uniform in strength, with both ends of the line extended and thinned out.

I also think Hannibal had his central line on the front shielded his African troops of both flanks. If the Romans can see clearly the opposing force had well defined left, center, and right sections, they would extend their battle line to match the whole front as I described earlier. Hannibal must deployed his formation as such that the flanking sections won't be easily engaged by Romans at the beginning of the battle. I think he had his central formation marched forward and stretched to cover the whole front. Here on the Figure 3 I have them semi-hidden behind the center front line. That means, Hannibal matched the width of Roman formation by overstretching his center section.

## the first contact

What would the Roman do once their front line came in contact with the crescent Carthaginian line? I don't think they can maintain a 2 km long straight line. Instead, the gradual curving of the crescent line will pull them inwards. Now imagine you are a frontline Roman soldier on the right wing. You can see and hear your brothers on your left fighting fiercely, and there is an enemy soldier standing two steps in front of you. Would you stand still? Or step forward to fight your enemy? I believe it is human's instinct and common sense to do the latter, especially for green troops in the heat of combat (Figure 4a). It happened across the whole front on both wings, and whole battle front of Roman infantry turned from a strand line to a concave shape (Figure 4b). It also explained why Hannibal can afford to thin out the flanking of the center unit: they don't need to hold Roman in place, only need to engage, and gradually withdraw while helding the continous line. On contrast, his cental companies will have to check the Roman advance initially, wait untill the whole line is engaged with Romans, and they have to have enough density and strength. As a straight line bend forward, the distance between the right-most soldier to the left-most soldier decreased. Therefore, the Roman soldiers on both flanks are now turning away from Hannibal's African troops after engaging with the crescent line. At last, the scale of this battle is too big for individual soldiers to understand what was happening. In my figure 4, the solders at both edge only turned ~30 degree. But the real battle field was much wider, ~2km long and only a few hundred meters deep, so the turning angle would be even smaller.

## Controlled withdrawal  

After the whole battle front was engaged, Hannibal's whole central front line pulled back toward the center in a controlled withdrawal, as seen in Figure 4B. The prevailing option that Hannibal held both wings only pulled back in the middle to form a pincer has several problems. 1, it negates the necessity of the crescent front. The crescent front is well-documented, and all historians emphasized it was essential to Hannibal's success. However, if the goal was to sink Romans in a pincer, the starting position does not matter. If Hannibal's infantry was so strong that can manipulate the shape of the front at will, they can just start as a flat line and do the same maneuver. 2, the Carthaginian infantry is not strong enough to withhold Roman legion's advance, as demonstrated in Trebia. As the Roman troops is much more numerous in Cannae, it would be very dangerous for Hannibal to bank his success on his infantry to held Roman infantry. If they tried the pincer movement as described, Roman infantry line will very likely breakthrough from the center. 3, the Carthagenian infantry line on both wings are too weak to held Roman line in place. When Hannibal deployed his crescent front, it has been descried that he stretched his line at both edge to match the wide front of the Romans. Overstretched, thinned out positions are potential weak points. Let's say Hannibal wanted to held both pincer and sink the Roman legions inside, he would have to use strong troops with firm positions hoping them can hold it, not with extended thin formation. He did have African troops deployed on both wings, but he did not use them to withheld Roman advance, only used them after the battle front was shaped. When Hannibal extend his crescent line at the edges, his intent was clear: he did not expect them to held their position, but to engage and then pull back.

## What did Hannibal's tactical move do to the Roman formation

With the battle started, the Roman soldiers must feel really good about the battle. They are attacking and advancing, especially units deployed on both wings. Some of them may felt the whole front tilted, but still no big deal. Remember, none of them had a bird sight view of the battle field, and they had no idea their advance had gradually bent and turned inward.

At the beginning of the battle, the Roman formation was optimally spaced for effective combat, while the central Carthaginian formation was overstreched and thinned out. As the Carthaginian troops pulled back toward the center they gradually shrunk the width of battle front. In the process their overextended line became condensed while the Roman formation gradually, but inevitably became overcrowded. I don't think the 2km front shrink uniformly, but the whole width of the Roman line was decided the distance between the leftmost and right most soldier. Once they locked-in the combat with the opposing enemy and attack forward, the manuver of units in the middle did not matter. First, the space between manples disappeared. Then the distance between shoulders became smaller and smaller. Even if some units tried to maintain discipline, the other units on the left and right would keep crushing into their space. Without a unified command to pull some units back, there was not much an individual unit can do.

Command and control of large formation is hard. Roman infantry got their order to advance, and they have the discipline to maintain the continuation of their line and not to flee. What happened was inevitable once they engage the crescent line and marched forward. On the hindsight, they should not deploy all their troops at once, or they would need a follow-up order to pull some of the troops off the front line and turn to the flanks, but the Roman commander was leading the charge on the front line (just like Trebia) and again, nobody had a bird sight view of the whole battle field. It was not a video game, nobody can select units and change their attack directions.

## Deploy the African troops

With the battle front shrunk to a desirable width, Hannibal ordered his African troops on both sides to attack and fix the front width. Meanwhile, his cavalry started attack Roman infantry from the rear. The timing of these movement is critical, since the Roman commander would eventually realize what had happened and reorganize (pull units from front line to face rear or flanks, etc.). Hannibal's flanking units and cavalry has to attack and engage Roman troops in time to eliminate any space for Romans to reorganize, but they (especially troops on the flank) had to wait till the the front shrunk.

The positioning of the African troops also makes sense now if the battle unfolded as described above: they can hide behind the central line first, and directly attack both flanks of Roman formation after the front shrunk.

## The Romans were too disciplined to flee

The final battle situation are shown in the Figure 5. The Carthaginian did not have the luxury to deploy overwhelming forces on all four directions, but they don't need to. Just like Trebia, Roman infantry kept pushing harder and harder forward, but unlike Trebia, the harder they push, the formation became denser, ultimately their formation became too crowded to fight effectively.

![Fig. 5: The final position](/assets/img/Cannae_Fig5.jpg){: width="750" }

But why didn't they change direction, attack the Carthaginian calvary and try to make it back to their camp? I think they would escape such a crushing defeat and annihilation if they did that, but it was not a video game that you can just circle all the troops and change attack direction with a click. After Hannibal's African troops closed in from both flanks, the Roman commander might realize what had happened, but their formation had became too dense to maneuver effectively. Facing calvary on their rear, sparse fleeing infantry soldiers would be hunted down and their only chance to survive was to move in a dense formation, like we have seen numerous times during the history of Rome. However, without a unified command from the top, the legions would carry on the last order they received: continue their attack forward.

## Conclusion

So there it is, my understanding of how battle of cannae unfold. I think it makes more sense tactically. It explains why the crescent front is so essential for Hannibal's total victory, why Hannibal streched the flanking line thinner, Polybius's description that Romans "pressing on to the centre", and also why Roman formation got overcrowded.

But it does require us to understand the crescent-to-concave moneuver a little different: if you include the flanking African troops, the Carthage line did generally change from crescent to concave shape.

As I said in the beginning, Cannae was such an important and unique battle. The tactics employed by Hannibal, and the mind blowing outcome have never been repeated in the world's history. But we are so far removed from the ancient cold weapon melee, while our understanding of the command and control of ancient battles are simutanously compromised by video games. My description here may be different from the general consensus, but I believe I am right.
